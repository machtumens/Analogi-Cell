Cell biology / International Space Station

A cell works like a space station

Both are sealed volumes keeping something alive somewhere that would otherwise kill it. Both run on the same handful of jobs: hold the boundary, make power, build parts, ship them, take out the trash. Here they are side by side, one job at a time.

01 Boundary

Cell membraneandhull and airlock

The membrane is a double layer of fat with proteins parked in it. It is picky rather than sealed: small things slip through, everything else needs a channel or a door.

The station's hull holds pressure the same way, and anything crossing it goes through the Quest airlock on purpose, with someone deciding.

Phospholipid bilayer, channel protein, receptor Pressure hull, shielding, Quest airlock

02 The medium

Cytoplasmandcabin atmosphere

Everything inside the cell sits in cytosol, a crowded fluid of water, salts and dissolved protein. It is not empty space, it is the thing that lets parts reach each other.

Inside the station that role goes to the air, kept breathable and pushed around by fans day and night. Stop the fans and the crew ends up in a pocket of their own exhaled carbon dioxide.

Cytosol with suspended organelles Cabin volume, ducting and circulation fan

03 Command

Nucleusandservice module

The nucleus holds the DNA behind a double membrane and never lets it leave. Instructions go out as copies, through pores in the wall.

Zvezda is where the station's command and control lives. The plans come from the ground, the module holds them, and the rest of the station works off the copies.

Nuclear envelope, pores, chromatin, nucleolus Zvezda service module

04 Manufacturing

Ribosomeandon-orbit 3D printer

A ribosome reads the instruction strand and builds a protein from it, one amino acid at a time. Tiny machine, enormous output.

The station's printer works off a file sent up from the ground and lays down a part in plastic. Same idea: instructions in, physical object out, no delivery needed.

Large and small subunits, mRNA, growing chain Additive manufacturing rack

05 Processing

Endoplasmic reticulumandlabs and nodes

New proteins land in the rough ER, which is studded with ribosomes, and get folded and checked before they go anywhere. The smooth ER next door handles fats and detox work.

The lab modules do the equivalent, and the nodes between them are the corridor that carries work from one bay to the next.

Rough cisternae and smooth tubules Destiny, Columbus and a connecting node

06 Shipping

Golgi complexandcargo module

The Golgi is a stack of flattened sacs that takes finished proteins, adds the last tags, and buds them off in vesicles addressed to wherever they are needed.

Leonardo is the station's packing room. Cargo is sorted, bagged, labelled and loaded onto whichever vehicle is leaving next.

Cisternae with budding vesicles Leonardo permanent multipurpose module

07 Power

Mitochondriaandsolar arrays

Mitochondria burn sugar and oxygen to make ATP, the small rechargeable currency every other part of the cell spends. The folds inside, the cristae, exist to pack in more surface area.

The arrays do the same trick with sunlight, and the batteries carry the station through the 45 minutes of darkness in every 90-minute orbit.

Outer membrane, cristae, ATP Solar wings, gimbal, batteries

08 Waste

Lysosomeandcargo freighter

Lysosomes are the cell's stomach: a membrane bag of enzymes that breaks down worn-out parts and returns the pieces for reuse. Keeping those enzymes bagged is the whole point.

Cygnus is the station's version. It arrives full of supplies, leaves full of trash, and burns up on the way down.

Enzymes digesting a worn-out organelle Cygnus freighter, loaded for disposal

09 Structure

Cytoskeletonandintegrated truss

The cytoskeleton is scaffolding and railway at once. Microtubules, actin and intermediate filaments hold the cell's shape and give motor proteins a track to haul cargo along.

The truss is the station's backbone, and the robotic arm rides a cart down its rail to wherever the work is.

Microtubules, actin, centrioles Truss segment, mobile transporter, arm

10 Ending on purpose

Apoptosisandplanned deorbit

Cells do not only die by accident. Apoptosis is a shutdown the cell runs on itself when it is damaged, infected or simply no longer needed: enzymes called caspases switch on, the chromatin condenses, and the cell packages itself into neat parcels that neighbours absorb. Nothing spills, nothing inflames.

The ISS gets the same kind of ending. Rather than letting a 435-tonne structure come down wherever it likes, the plan is to lower the orbit from 2028, dock a purpose-built deorbit vehicle, and steer the breakup into an empty stretch of the South Pacific around 2030. A controlled end is part of the design.

Blebbing cell and apoptotic bodies Station under retro burn, deorbit vehicle aft

Where the analogy stops: a cell builds its own replacements and copies itself, and no one on the ground is deciding anything. The station cannot do either.
